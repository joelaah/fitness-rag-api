## Compound Movements: The Big Four and Their Biomechanics

Compound movements are exercises that involve multiple joints and muscle groups working simultaneously. They form the foundation of effective strength training because they allow heavier loads, stimulate greater hormonal responses, and develop functional, real-world strength.

### The Barbell Squat

**Primary Muscles**: Quadriceps, Gluteus Maximus, Adductors.
**Secondary Muscles**: Hamstrings, Erector Spinae, Core stabilizers, Calves.

**Proper Form**: Feet shoulder-width apart or slightly wider, toes pointed out 15-30 degrees. Bar positioned on the upper traps (high bar) or rear delts (low bar). Descend by simultaneously bending at the hips and knees. Maintain a neutral spine throughout the movement. Depth should reach at least parallel (hip crease at or below knee level) for full quadricep and glute activation. Drive through the full foot, not just the heels. Knees should track over the toes — it is a myth that knees should never pass the toes.

**Common Errors**: Excessive forward lean (weak core or ankle mobility limitation), knee valgus (knees caving inward, indicating weak hip abductors), butt wink (posterior pelvic tilt at the bottom, often caused by limited hip flexor or hamstring flexibility), rising hips faster than shoulders out of the bottom (quad weakness relative to posterior chain).

**Recommended Rep Ranges**: Strength: 3-5 reps at 80-90% 1RM. Hypertrophy: 6-12 reps at 65-80% 1RM. Endurance: 12-20 reps at 50-65% 1RM.

### The Barbell Deadlift

**Primary Muscles**: Erector Spinae, Gluteus Maximus, Hamstrings.
**Secondary Muscles**: Quadriceps, Trapezius, Rhomboids, Forearms (grip), Latissimus Dorsi, Core.

**Proper Form (Conventional)**: Feet hip-width apart, bar over mid-foot. Grip just outside the knees (double overhand or mixed grip). Hips hinge back, chest up, shoulders slightly in front of the bar. Engage lats by pulling the slack out of the bar before lifting. Drive through the floor while maintaining the bar in contact with or very close to the shins and thighs. Lockout by squeezing the glutes and standing tall — do not hyperextend the lower back.

**Conventional vs. Sumo**: Sumo deadlift uses a wider stance (feet outside shoulder width) with hands inside the knees. Sumo reduces spinal shear force by approximately 10% and places more emphasis on hip abductors and quadriceps. Conventional deadlift has greater posterior chain emphasis. Choose based on individual hip anatomy and leverage.

**Safety**: Never round the lower back under load. If grip fails before the posterior chain, use straps for working sets and train grip separately with farmer's walks and dead hangs.

### The Barbell Bench Press

**Primary Muscles**: Pectoralis Major, Anterior Deltoids, Triceps.
**Secondary Muscles**: Serratus Anterior, Biceps (stabilizers), Core.

**Proper Form**: Retract and depress the shoulder blades (squeeze them together and down). Maintain a slight arch in the thoracic spine — this is a natural protective position, not dangerous. Grip width: hands 1.5x shoulder width apart. Lower the bar to the lower chest / sternum with controlled speed (2-3 second eccentric). Press up and slightly back toward the rack in a J-curve path. Feet flat on the floor, driving through leg drive to maintain upper back tightness.

**Common Errors**: Flaring elbows to 90 degrees (shoulder impingement risk — keep elbows at 45-75 degrees), bouncing the bar off the chest, losing scapular retraction, lifting hips off the bench.

**Variations**: Incline Bench Press (30-45 degree angle) emphasizes the upper pectorals and anterior deltoids. Decline Bench Press (15-30 degree decline) emphasizes the lower pectorals. Dumbbell Bench Press increases range of motion and unilateral stability demands.

### The Overhead Press (Standing Barbell Press)

**Primary Muscles**: Anterior and Lateral Deltoids, Triceps.
**Secondary Muscles**: Upper Trapezius, Serratus Anterior, Core (significant stabilization demand), Upper Pectoralis.

**Proper Form**: Stand with feet hip-width apart, slight bend in the knees. Bar starts on the front of the shoulders (front rack position). Brace the core and squeeze the glutes. Press the bar straight up, moving the head slightly back to clear the chin, then forward once the bar passes. Lock out directly overhead with the bar over the mid-foot. Avoid excessive backward lean — this turns the press into an incline bench and loads the lumbar spine.

**Why the Overhead Press Matters**: It is the most demanding upper body compound lift for shoulder stability and core strength. A strong overhead press correlates strongly with healthy shoulder function and injury resistance in overhead athletes.

**Progression**: The overhead press is the slowest upper body lift to progress. Adding 2.5 lbs (1 kg) per session is realistic for beginners, and many intermediate lifters will progress only 2.5-5 lbs per month.
