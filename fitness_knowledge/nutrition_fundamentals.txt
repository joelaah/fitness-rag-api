## Nutrition Fundamentals for Strength Training

Nutrition is the fuel for recovery and adaptation. Without adequate nutrition, even the best training program will produce suboptimal results. The three primary nutritional considerations for strength training are: total caloric intake, macronutrient distribution, and meal timing.

### Caloric Requirements

**Maintenance Calories**: The number of calories needed to maintain current body weight. Calculate using: Body weight in lbs × 14-16 (sedentary to active) or Body weight in kg × 30-35.

**Caloric Surplus (Muscle Gain)**: To build muscle optimally, consume 250-500 calories above maintenance daily. This supports approximately 0.5-1 lb (0.25-0.5 kg) of weight gain per week. A surplus larger than 500 calories does not accelerate muscle growth — it primarily increases fat gain.

**Caloric Deficit (Fat Loss)**: To lose fat while preserving muscle, maintain a deficit of 300-500 calories below maintenance. Larger deficits (over 750 calories) increase muscle loss risk, reduce training performance, and impair recovery. During a cut, maintain training intensity (load on the bar) and reduce volume by 30-40% if recovery is compromised.

**Body Recomposition**: Beginners, detrained individuals, and those with high body fat can simultaneously build muscle and lose fat by eating at maintenance or a slight deficit while training hard and consuming adequate protein.

### Protein

Protein is the most critical macronutrient for muscle repair and growth.

**Daily Protein Target**: 1.6-2.2 g per kg of body weight (0.7-1.0 g per lb). A meta-analysis by Morton et al. (2018) concluded that 1.6 g/kg/day was the threshold above which additional protein provided diminishing returns for hypertrophy.

**Protein Distribution**: Distribute protein intake across 3-5 meals, consuming 20-40g per meal. This maximizes muscle protein synthesis (MPS) throughout the day. A single dose of 20-25g of high-quality protein maximally stimulates MPS in young adults; 40g is recommended for older adults and larger individuals.

**Protein Quality**: Complete proteins containing all essential amino acids are superior. Leucine content is the primary trigger for MPS. Best sources:
- Whey protein: 11% leucine, rapidly absorbed. Ideal post-workout.
- Eggs: Complete amino acid profile, high bioavailability.
- Chicken breast: 31g protein per 100g, lean.
- Greek yogurt: 10g protein per 100g, includes casein for sustained release.
- Beef: 26g protein per 100g, rich in creatine and iron.
- Fish (salmon, tuna): 20-25g protein per 100g, includes omega-3 fatty acids.
- Legumes + grains (for plant-based athletes): Combine to achieve complete amino acid profiles.

**Pre-Sleep Protein**: Consuming 30-40g of casein protein (or cottage cheese) 30-60 minutes before bed increases overnight MPS by 22% without increasing fat storage, according to research by Snijders et al. (2015).

### Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates fuel high-intensity exercise and replenish muscle glycogen stores.

**Daily Carbohydrate Target**: 3-7 g per kg body weight depending on training volume and intensity.
- Light training (3 days/week): 3-4 g/kg
- Moderate training (4-5 days/week): 4-5 g/kg
- High volume training (6 days/week): 5-7 g/kg

**Timing**: Consume the majority of carbohydrates around training sessions. Pre-workout meal (1-3 hours before) should include 30-60g of complex carbohydrates. Post-workout meal should include 40-80g of carbohydrates to replenish glycogen within the 2-hour recovery window.

**Carbohydrate Sources**: Prioritize complex, minimally processed sources: oats, rice, sweet potatoes, whole grain bread, fruits, quinoa, and legumes. Simple sugars (fruit juice, dextrose) are appropriate immediately post-workout for rapid glycogen replenishment.

### Fats

Dietary fat is essential for hormone production (especially testosterone), joint health, and vitamin absorption.

**Daily Fat Target**: 0.7-1.2 g per kg body weight, or 20-35% of total calories.

**Fat Sources**: Prioritize monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fats: olive oil, avocado, nuts (almonds, walnuts), fatty fish (salmon, mackerel), seeds (chia, flax). Limit saturated fat to under 10% of total calories. Avoid trans fats entirely.

**Omega-3 Fatty Acids**: Consume 2-3g of EPA+DHA daily through fatty fish (2-3 servings per week) or fish oil supplementation. Omega-3s reduce exercise-induced inflammation, improve joint health, and may enhance muscle protein synthesis.

### Hydration for Performance

**Daily Water Intake**: Minimum 0.5 oz per pound of body weight (approximately 30-35 mL per kg). Active individuals should increase to 0.7-1.0 oz per pound.

**During Training**: Drink 7-10 oz (200-300 mL) every 10-20 minutes during exercise. For sessions exceeding 60 minutes, include electrolytes (sodium, potassium).

**Dehydration Impact**: A 2% loss in body weight from fluid loss reduces strength by 2%, power by 3%, and endurance capacity by up to 10%.

### Key Supplements (Evidence-Based)

**Creatine Monohydrate**: The most researched and effective supplement for strength and power. Take 3-5g daily (no loading phase necessary). Increases intramuscular phosphocreatine stores, improving high-intensity exercise capacity by 5-15%.

**Caffeine**: 3-6 mg per kg body weight, consumed 30-60 minutes before training. Improves strength, endurance, and perceived effort. Avoid within 8 hours of bedtime to protect sleep quality.

**Vitamin D**: 2000-5000 IU daily if blood levels are below 40 ng/mL. Essential for bone health, immune function, and testosterone production. Deficiency is common in indoor athletes and those in northern latitudes.
